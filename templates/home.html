Frequently Asked Questions
APPLYING FOR AID
Start by visiting the official FAFSA® website at studentaid.gov. You'll need to create an FSA ID for yourself and one for a parent. The application is free and asks for financial information about your family. Many states use your FAFSA information to determine eligibility for state aid, but some may have a separate application. Check your state's education agency website for specific requirements.
Eligibility depends on your financial need, academic merit, and other factors. The main types are: Grants (free money, usually need-based), Scholarships (free money, usually merit-based), Work-Study (earned money from a part-time job), and Federal Loans (borrowed money you must repay). Filing the FAFSA is the first step to finding out what you're eligible for.
The only way to know for sure is to apply! Most families qualify for some form of aid. The process starts with the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA®) at studentaid.gov. This single application determines your eligibility for federal grants, loans, and work-study, and is also used by most states and colleges to award their own aid.
MANAGING YOUR MONEY
Begin by listing all your income sources (work, family help, financial aid). Then, track all your expenses for a month, categorizing them into needs (tuition, housing, food) and wants (entertainment, dining out). Use a simple spreadsheet or budgeting app to see where your money goes. The key is to ensure your expenses don't exceed your income. Our Budget Planner tool can help you get started!
Minimize debt by prioritizing grants and scholarships first. If you must borrow, only take what you absolutely need and choose federal loans over private ones for better terms. Create a budget to understand your repayment obligations before you graduate. Once in repayment, consider an income-driven repayment plan to keep payments manageable.
Be cautious of anyone who guarantees you a scholarship or charges a fee for financial aid help—the FAFSA is always free. Stick to official government websites (.gov) for federal aid. For loans, exhaust your federal options first, as they have better protections and lower fixed rates than most private loans. Never share your FSA ID or personal financial information with unsolicited sources.
SPECIAL GROUPS & TOPICS
Many colleges have dedicated programs for first-generation students offering mentorship, academic support, and community-building events. National organizations like I'm First!, QuestBridge, and Trio provide valuable resources, scholarships, and support networks. Don't hesitate to ask your college's student affairs office what specific resources they offer.
Yes, absolutely! Many organizations and colleges offer scholarships specifically for first-generation or immigrant students to promote diversity and access to education. Use scholarship search engines and filter for "first-generation" or your specific background. Organizations like TheDream.US and the Jack Kent Cooke Foundation have excellent programs.
While undocumented and DACA students are not eligible for federal aid, many states and colleges offer their own financial aid. Some states allow you to file a state-specific application instead of the FAFSA. Additionally, you can apply for private scholarships—many do not have citizenship requirements. Check your state's laws and your college's financial aid office for details.
